Beyond the Black Box: Fair, Explainable
Hate Speech Detection
Advait Surendra Mulmule, Devu Gupta, Sneha Rathod, Sai Sri Vaidehi Guntury, V. Ramya Varshini

Abstract—The world of communication has been reshaped by social media platforms and now
more than ever people are able to share and connect globally with minimal barrier. Yet this
omnipresent connectivity had brought some very intricate societal issues, it was the explosion
of online hate speech. Thus, in this paper, we propose a novel, robust and interpretable
framework for hate speech detection using the imbalanced HateXplain dataset. Our model
combines rationale supervision with a deep neural architecture that integrates
domain-adapted transformers and multi-kernel convolutional layers. After extensive training
on large cross-platform dataset, our model attained the top performance, with a macro F1
score of 0.89 and a hate-class F1 score of 0.86, and the quality of model-generated rationales
was in good accordance with human judgement and the amount of demographic bias
diminished. Put simply, the tool helps keep online spaces safer by catching hateful content
rapidly and accurately. It even reveals which words triggered the flag, so moderators can make
fair, transparent decisions rather than just trusting a black box. And because it looks for bias
between communities, rather than within communities, it mitigates against unfair targeting of
dialects and minority voices. As it scales up to more languages and platforms, it’s the kind of
tool that could help foster healthier, more respectful conversations across the internet.
Index Terms–Hate Speech Detection, Explainable AI, HateXplain Dataset, Transformer,
Convolutional Neural Networks, Rationale Supervision, Fairness.

I.​INTRODUCTION
The framework for human connection is now fundamentally changed, thanks to the digital revolution.
“What started as very crude networks for people staying in touch turned into the main platform for
global political conversation, cultural exchange and community building,” said Mr. Still, this unequaled
democratization of voice has come at an extraordinary cost: the systemic propagation of digital toxicity.
Growing in size, these platforms have come to foster hate speech – language that attacks, denigrates or
calls for violence against people based on protected attributes, such as race, religion, gender and sexual
orientation. It is not just a technical annoyance, but a profound social crisis affecting social cohesion
and bodily [1] safety. The rate of content production, now in the millions of posts per minute has made
traditional manual content moderation methods somewhat obsolete. Consequently the responsibility
for policing this digital public square has increasingly shifted toward automated systems, driving
forward the development of algorithms that are capable of detecting the complex, vitriolic and
occasionally sophisticated language of online hate [21], [36].
The journey toward effective automated moderation has been marked by a continuous arms race
between detection technologies and the evolving language of abuse. Historically, the field relied on
rigid, feature-engineering approaches. Early systems utilized Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and
Random Forests, dependent on static "blocklists" of profanity and slurs [6]. While computationally

efficient, these "Bag-of-Words" models suffered from a catastrophic lack of nuance. They treated
language as a simple collection of tokens, failing to distinguish between a slur used in a hateful attack
and one used in a reclaimed or educational context [18].
The beginning of Deep Learning was a huge paradigm shift. The development of RNN and LSTM
networks enabled models to be oriented to the sequential nature of the text and capture the
inter-dependencies among words within the entire sentence that contributes to the syntax and
semantics of the sentence [13]. However, the real game changer was the Transformer architecture.
Models such as BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) and RoBERTa
transformed the space, reading text bidirectionally through self-attention mechanisms [5]. It
introduced the ability to "re-read" the context, as systems could now distinguish between different
word senses for example.
To fine-tune these capabilities, the state-of-the-art has gradually shifted towards hybrid models. While
Transformers excel at grasping global context, researchers observed that they might "over-smooth"
very local patterns of interest. By integrating Transformers with Convolutional Neural Networks
(CNNs)—traditionally used for image processing—researchers discovered they could capture both the
overall meaning of a post and the specific, local n-gram clusters (such as "go back to") that act as strong
indicators of hate speech [10]. Recent works prove that such combined BERT-CNN models achieve
superior performance, effectively fusing high-level semantic features with low-level n-gram patterns
[19]. Moreover, advances in multi-kernel convolutions and capsule networks have enabled the
extraction of even finer-grained features, such as variable-length linguistic patterns, which are
imperative for capturing abuse in the noisy and abbreviated language of tweets [9], [15].
Yet, even as these architectural changes have borne fruit, the use of AI in high-stakes social moderation
remains riddled with ethical and technical hazards. As models have become larger and more complex,
they have become less transparent—"black boxes." A modern deep learning classifier might state with
99% probability that a post is hateful, but it cannot explain why. In a democratic society, where
removing content is tantamount to a restriction of speech, this lack of explainability is a crucial
impediment to trust and accountability [1], [11].
Even more alarming is the issue of algorithmic bias. Since these models are often trained on
massive amounts of data taken from the internet without any censoring, they tend to mirror (and
amplify) the biases in society that were present in that data [4]. "Identity term bias" is a well known
failure mode, where models become trained to associate neutral terms relating to marginalized groups
(i.e. "Muslim," "Gay," "Black") as toxic language. This makes for an unacceptably high false positive rate
in content written about or by these communities, and in practice silencing the voices that the tools are
meant to protect. For example, African American Vernacular English (AAVE) is more likely to be
flagged as hateful by off-the-shelf classifiers, further demonstrating the need for bias reduction
methods that disentangle dialect and identity from hatefulness [20], [24].
The issue of latent hate is also largely unaddressed. Explicit slurs are easy for models to identify, but
sarcasm, metaphor or dog whistles — code words intended only to be understood within a particular
community [22]. There’s no profanity in a sentence like “You belong in the kitchen,” but it’s
unquestionably gender-based harassment -.. Traditional black-box models may not be able to capture
subtleties, and thus there may be a high false-negative rate of subtle but harmful abuse [17], [32].
In this paper, we present a comprehensive framework designed to bridge these gaps. We propose a
Rationale-Supervised HateBERT-CNN, a novel architecture that synergizes three specific technological
advancements to create a system that is not only accurate but also fair and explainable.

First, we address the domain gap through Domain Adaptation. Standard BERT models are trained on
polite, formal text like Wikipedia, leaving them ill-equipped to parse the slang and aggression of social
media. We utilize HateBERT [36], a model that has undergone "continued pre-training" on a large
corpora of banned and toxic comments. This step teaches the model the specific vocabulary of online
abuse before it attempts classification [5].
Second, we integrate a Multi-Kernel CNN to act as a specialized feature extractor. By operating on the
rich embeddings provided by HateBERT, this component scans for local linguistic patterns at various
granularities (bi-grams, tri-grams), ensuring that short, punchy phrases characteristic of hate speech
are detected alongside the broader context [10], [27].
Third, and most critically, we introduce Rationale Supervision. Unlike traditional training, which only
penalizes the model for getting the final label wrong, our approach utilizes the HateXplain dataset [37]
to supervise the model's attention. We force the model to align its internal focus with
human-annotated "rationales"—the specific segments of text that human reviewers identified as the
reason for the hate label. This dual-loss training objective penalizes the model if it focuses on irrelevant
bias words (like "gay") instead of the actual hateful context. This supervision acts as a powerful bias
mitigation tool, teaching the model how to reason like a human [1], [8].
This study adds to the increasing literature promoting "fairness-by-design" in the field of Natural
Language Processing [3], [28]. Our work shows that the trade-off between model accuracy and
interpretability is a false paradox—by basing our model on human rationales, we achieve
state-of-the-art performance (Macro F1 0.89) while substantially reducing demographic bias.
The framework presented here provides a feasible roadmap for human-in-the-loop moderation. By
producing faithful rationales along with predictions, our model allows human experts to make quicker
and more informed decisions on toxic content, significantly reducing the cognitive burden on
assessors. Ultimately, this paper advocates for the development of a holistic detection ecosystem—one
that is robust enough to pick up subtle hate, fair enough to avoid disproportionately silencing
marginalized voices, and transparent enough to foster user trust [7], [12].

II.​LITERATURE REVIEW
Interest in automated hate speech detection has gone through a phase in which theoretical study
dominated to the point where it is now firmly rooted in practical considerations and deployment in
real-world scenarios. The recent research work 2023~2025 sheds a clear pattern of convergence
towards hybrid neural architectures in which semantic representations delivered by Transformers
(such as BERT, RoBERTa) are complemented/ enhanced by local feature extraction mechanisms (e.g.,
Convolutional Neural Network (CNNs) and Capsule Networks) in order to handle the large noise in
informal language style on social media text [2], [8]. High-performance models are now more opaque
which has led to the introduction of Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, and there is a growing tendency
among researchers to value rationale-based supervision to make decisions not only accurate, but
interpretable and fair [1], [3]. The area of detection has also expanded to tackle major challenges such
as enabling multilingual generalization for low-resource languages and reducing identity bias for
under-represented communities [5], [16]. To systematically analyze this burgeoning field, we
summarize in Table I the key works examined in this article along with the specific authors, methods
employed, quantitative measures (F1-scores, Accuracy), and recent critical research gaps from

Although great advances have been made in automated detection of hate speech, a crucial research
gap remains to be filled: the competing interests of model performance and trustworthy explainability,
especially when dealing with implicit hate and dialectal bias [17], [22]. The current state-of-the-art
approaches tend to be opaque "black box" models, which achieve a high accuracy by memorizing
identity terms and dataset-specific artifacts and do not really learn how to distinguish targeted hate
from general offensive or benign conversational [3], [24]. On the other hand, those that focus on
interpretability tend to be computationally inefficient or less predictive, for they are frequently
dependent on resource-demanding LLMs and therefore not amenable to real-time deployment [1], [6].
We directly address this stalemate in our study by introducing the Rationale-Supervised

[36]

HateBERT-CNN model. By combining domain-adapted embeddings with multi-scale feature extraction
and more importantly by constraining the learning procedure with human labeled rationales, we
impose a rationale to let the accuracy be ruled by causal linguistic evidence instead of spurious
correlations [8], [10]. This paper offers a solid, “fairness-by-design” end-to-end solution to content
moderation for those who will build it next — one that detects toxicity with the kind of precision
needed to back human moderators, providing transparent rationale of detection, making it possible to
develop AI systems that protect, rather than police, marginalized voices in the digital commons [5],
[11].

This section describes our overall approach to hate speech detection, divided into five major parts:
data collection and preprocessing, model architecture design, choice of loss function, training
procedures, and evaluation strategy. Figure 1 shows the entire pipeline of the proposed approach, from
raw social media posts through HateBERT embedding, multi-kernel CNN feature extraction,
rationale-supervised attention, to classification outputs with explainable rationales.

A.​

Data Collection

A.1. Dataset Selection
To train our models, we rely on the HateXplain benchmark dataset [37] that has been recently
validated for its effectiveness as a contributor to Explainable AI (XAI) in content moderation [1], [17] in
the literature. Previous works had to be discarded due to vast label noise and demographic skew —
problems that had been highlighted in an empirical evaluation of public datasets by Jaf and Barakat [3]
— however, HateXplain is a crowdsourced high-quality rationale annotated dataset consisting of
19,229 posts on social media from two platforms, Twitter and Gab. This bi-platform dataset captures
both diversification in language (Twitter is standard micro-blogging syntax, Gab engages in
radicalized, echo-chamber style discourse.)
There are a number of reasons why we chose HateXplain over the single-source or pure
classification-based datasets. Firstly, its balanced class composition alleviates the “majority class bias”
problem in existing large-scale scrapes, wherein models are trained to predict “non-hate” as a default
class given the limited number of hateful samples [4], [2] Human-annotated rationales (ground truth
evidence) are provided, allowing the use of supervised attention. As shown in Salles et al. [8] and
Nishimura [17], redirecting model attention to mirror human reasoning is an essential step in making
sure that predictions rely on causal linguistic evidence rather than arbitrary correlations. In addition,
explicit target community labels enable rigorous fairness audit, which addresses the recent call for
biased measurements for and against particular demographic groups [11].
A.2. Dataset characteristics
For each post, HateXplain provides three granular annotation layers that facilitate both robust
detection and interpretable reasoning:
1.​
Classification Labels: Messages are categorized into one of three mutually exclusive classes:
hate speech, offensive language, or normal. These labels are derived via majority voting among three
independent annotators, a consensus approach that significantly reduces the label noise and annotator
disagreement inherent in single-rater datasets [7].
2.​
Token-Level Rationales: The dataset includes span-level annotations where annotators
highlight the specific words or phrases (e.g., slurs, violent verbs, or stereotyping adjectives) that justify
the classification. These rationales are essential for training "glass-box" models capable of explaining
their decisions, moving beyond the opacity of traditional black-box classifiers [1].
3.​
Target Community Labels: Metadata identifying the specific group targeted by the abuse (e.g.,
Race, Gender, Sexual Orientation) is provided. This layer enables fine-grained performance analysis
and is crucial for evaluating model fairness across different identity groups [11].
The class distribution of the data is balanced in a manner that significantly enhances model learning
for discriminative features of each category, which had a severe under-representation of hate speech
(often <6%). Key characteristics of the dataset, including the specific counts for each class and split, are
shown in Table II and Figure 1..

B.2. Text Processing Pipeline
Intuition from the domain motivated us to keep the preprocessing minimal so that semantic and
emotional signals could be preserved. We did the following:
1.​
Retention of linguistic features: Punctuation, emojis and abbreviations were retained as they
convey important semantic content and emotional information which is important for processing in
hate speech classification.
2.​
Tokenization: We tokenized posts using the HateBERT tokenizer so that the domain-specific
vocabulary and hate speech terms are properly encoded and represented.
3.​
Sequence Normalization: We padded or truncated every post to 512 tokens, as this is
a common length for transformer models and provides good trade-off between computational cost
and amount of context.

C.​

Model Architecture and Implementation

Our proposed architecture brings together three complementary components designed to improve
classification accuracy and interpretability. Model development and experimentation were conducted
entirely using the PyTorch deep learning library, given its flexibility and popularity for advanced
natural language processing research. This modular PyTorch-based approach gives us the ability to
reproduce experimentation and model tuning while harnessing both the domain expertise of
domain-adapted transformers[5] and the pattern extraction power of the multi-kernel convolutional
architecture [10], [27].
C.1. HateBERT: Domain-Specialized Transformer Encoder
Basically, the architecture of our bot relies on HateBERT, a BERT Model adapted to the hate speech
domain. Unlike fine-tuned language models that are only pre-trained with general language on large
amounts of data such as Wikipedia, HateBERT has been further pre-trained on the RAL-E dataset,
consisting of over one million posts obtained from banned Reddit communities dedicated to offensive
and hateful material [5], [9]. This allows the model to have a domain aware word distribution as well
as contextual understanding of toxicity which standard models do not have.
Pre-training in particular allows the model to learn representations of "coded language", slurs and
extremist language which is commonly used to circumvent keyword based filters [9]. Studies recently
on the Sensitivity of LLMs for Implicit Hate Speech Detection [22] and LLM Sensitivity [24] suggest that
typical models do not perform well against these subtle linguistic features; however, HateBERT’s
training on this particular data distribution leads to HateBERT being significantly more robust to these
adversarial perturbations. The encoder takes as input tweets of varying lengths and transforms them
into a dense, context-aware embedding matrix, which is fed into our multi-kernel convolution layers in
the downstream pipeline. This is evidenced by current research stating that domain-specific

pretraining allows a 3-8% F1 score improvement in hate speech detection tasks[6]. Table IV shows a
detailed comparison of HateBERT with the standard baseline, highlighting the architectural and
training levers which led to largest gains in this domain.

C.2. Multi-Kernel Convolutional Layer
To supplement the global contextual understanding provided by HateBERT, we integrate a multi-kernel
convolutional architecture designed to capture local linguistic patterns at various granularities. Recent
studies in 2024 by Putra and Wang [10] and Sharif et al. [13] have empirically demonstrated that while
Transformers excel at semantic dependency, they can occasionally overlook short, discriminative
phrasal structures (n-grams) characteristic of hate speech. By applying parallel convolutions, we
effectively extract these "local hate signatures"—such as aggressive bigrams or specific slurs—that
operate at the word level, a technique further validated by Rahim and Mustapha [19] in their hybrid
Transformer-CNN models.
We employ four parallel convolutional layers with distinct kernel sizes: 2, 3, 4, and 5. Each kernel slides
over the HateBERT embedding matrix to extract features corresponding to specific linguistic scopes.

V.​DISCUSSIONS
Our research addresses four systemic failure modes that continue to plague real-world content
moderation—data skew, decision opacity, dialectal fragility, and architectural inefficiency—by
synthesizing a balanced, rationale-annotated dataset (HateXplain) with a Rationale-Supervised
HateBERT-CNN. Concretely, our system yields a Macro F1 of 0.89 (Table IX) and a Hate Class Recall of
0.88, alongside a token-level rationale agreement of 0.64. These metrics indicate not just high accuracy,
but reliable accuracy that is grounded in linguistic evidence rather than dataset artifacts.
A. Data Quality and The Artifact Problem
Recent audits by Jaf and Barakat [3] and Abusaqer et al. [6] have heavily criticized the field’s reliance
on older, noisy datasets like the Davidson corpus. They demonstrated that high F1 scores in previous
studies were often illusory, driven by models overfitting to specific keywords (e.g., "trash") or
annotator disagreement artifacts. By training on the HateXplain dataset [37], which utilizes a
near-balanced split (~32/41/27) and consensus-based labeling, we eliminate the "majority class bias"
that Ghosh et al. [4] identified as a primary cause of poor minority-class recall in low-resource settings.
Unlike models trained on skewed scrapes that learn to simply predict "Not Hate," our high recall (0.88)
proves the model has learned robust, discriminative features of toxicity.
B. Architectural Superiority: Why Hybrid?
While Putra and Wang [10] demonstrated the viability of BERT-CNN architectures, their model
achieved an F1 of only 0.73 on standard benchmarks. Our model outperforms this baseline by +16
points (0.89). This performance leap stems from two critical design choices:
1.​
Domain Adaptation (HateBERT): As noted in Albladi et al.’s [5] review, standard BERT models
struggle with the "lexical shift" of online slang. By utilizing HateBERT, which is pre-trained on banned
Reddit communities, our encoder arrives with an intrinsic understanding of coded language that
standard BERT lacks.
2.​
Multi-Kernel Feature Extraction: Kamal et al.[9] argued for Capsule Networks to capture spatial
hierarchies, but found them computationally heavy. Our Multi-Kernel CNN (kernels 2–5) provides a
lightweight alternative that effectively captures the same variable-length phrasal patterns (e.g., "go
back to") without the latency overhead. This allows our model to detect short, punchy hate speech that
pure Transformers might over-smooth.
C. The Explainability-Latency Trade-off
A major trend in 2025, exemplified by Hashir et al. [1] (TARGE) and Yang et al. [28] (HARE), is the use
of Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate natural language explanations. While these systems offer

high plausibility, they suffer from two fatal flaws: high latency and hallucination. An LLM might
generate a convincing reason for a flag that has no basis in the text. In contrast, our
Rationale-Supervised Attention mechanism forces the model to highlight existing evidence tokens. This
ensures faithfulness—the explanation is the actual mathematical cause of the prediction—and
maintains an inference speed suitable for real-time moderation (~87ms/post), solving the efficiency
bottleneck identified in Zhang et al.’s [24] critique of LLM sensitivity.
D. Fairness and Implicit Hate
Ahn et al. [22] and Nishimura[17] highlight that "implicit hate" (sarcasm, humor) remains the hardest
category to detect, often requiring shared semantic knowledge. Standard "black box" models
frequently fail here, or worse, rely on "identity term bias" (flagging "gay" as hateful), as shown in Salles
et al. [8]. By supervising our model with human rationales, we explicitly penalize it for focusing on
identity terms unless they are modified by toxic adjectives. This "alignment" process reduces false
positives on dialectal speech (e.g., AAVE), addressing the fairness concerns, and ensures that our high
performance is achieved equitably across target groups.

VI.​CONCLUSIONS & LIMITATIONS
In this paper, we propose a novel Rationale-Supervised HateBERT-CNN model that integrates the two
aspects of domain-adapted pre-training and the feature extraction based on multi-kernel in one layer.
Grounding the attention of the model in human-annotated rationales, we obtain a Macro F1-score of
0.89 and a hate-class F1-score of 0.86 on a balanced, large-scale dataset. In addition to classification
performance, our temporal analysis demonstrates that online hate speech continues to be heavily
focused on ethnicity, gender, and nationality. Targeted abuse relating to sexual orientation and
ethnicity has been a consistent and worrying trend throughout the period 2020-2023, and whilst the
overall number of toxic submissions has varied over that period, so too must moderation strategies
adapt to be equally as dynamic and responsive to that evolving challenge.
Nonetheless, there are limitations to this study. Deep learning models’ performances on this task are
still vulnerable to the enduring challenge of class imbalance, which could affect the recall of the
low-frequency hate labels even in curated datasets. Besides, the method is designed for
English-language content. To accommodate the global nature of social media, architecture in further
studies has to be developed to cover multilingual and code-mixed scenarios (e.g. Hindi-English or
Roman Urdu) which are linguistically very challenging. Moreover, as digital abuse is increasingly
morphing into visual mediums, adding multimodal feature fusion modules to process memes and
video will be a vital direction for future research.
A. Implications for Practitioners
For platform administrators and AI practitioners, this research provides a real-world roadmap for
moving from “black-box” moderation to “glass-box” moderation.
●​
Trust and Transparency: This framework allows human moderators to better understand why
content was flagged by producing faithful token-level rationales along with predictions. This
transparency removes a major cognitive burden from reviewers and adds muscles to the users’ appeal
process.

●​
Bias Mitigation: The rationale supervised training objective introduces a clean tool for
mitigating “identity term bias”. Practitioners focused on this can apply this approach to make sure their
automated tools aren't silencing marginalized groups disproportionately when treating identity
markers as inherently toxic.
●​
Efficiency at scale: Contrary to generative LLMs which are notorious for being slow and may
hallucinate, our hybrid design achieves the necessary computational efficiency to operate in real-time
on a high throughput moderation pipeline with the semantic completeness required to capture implicit
hate space.
And in the end this work makes our digital commons safer by recapitulating a detection system that is
accurate, fair, and interpretable thereby elevating the benchmark for ethical AI in social computing.
